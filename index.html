About the physics

What Scatter computes, and what it leaves out

Light scattering spectroscopy (LSS) reads the size of cell nuclei from the colour of the light they send back, without stains and without resolving them. Scatter simulates that measurement end to end: exact Mie scattering for every nucleus, a noisy polarization-gated acquisition, and a least-squares inversion against precomputed Mie spectra.

1. The idea

Epithelial nuclei are the largest scatterers in the cell and are slightly denser than the cytoplasm around them. In dysplasia, the precursor of most carcinomas, they grow from roughly 5–6 µm to 10 µm and more, crowd together and take up more chromatin. Pathologists see this on a stained biopsy. Perelman and co-workers showed that the same information is carried by light singly backscattered from the intact tissue: its spectrum has a fine structure, periodic in inverse wavelength, whose frequency grows with nuclear size [1], and it can be used to detect dysplasia in vivo [3] and to image it [4].

2. One nucleus: Mie theory

Each nucleus is treated as a homogeneous sphere of diameter d and relative refractive index m = nnucleus / nmedium in a medium of index nmedium = 1.36. Its scattering is computed with the full Lorenz–Mie series, using a TypeScript port of the BHMIE algorithm of Bohren and Huffman [5]: logarithmic derivative by downward recurrence, Riccati–Bessel functions by upward recurrence, series truncated after x + 4x1/3 + 2 terms, where x = π d nmedium / λ is the size parameter (20 to 160 here). No approximation of the Rayleigh–Gans or van de Hulst type is used in the simulation.

The code is checked by unit tests against the sample output printed in Bohren and Huffman's Appendix A and against Wiscombe's reference cases [6] (efficiencies and asymmetry parameter to six significant figures, for size parameters from 0.055 to 10,000 and for dielectric, absorbing and metal-like spheres), and against the Rayleigh and anomalous-diffraction limits.

3. What the instrument collects

Scatter models polarized LSS [2][4]. The tissue is illuminated with linearly polarized white light and the backscattered light is analysed parallel and perpendicular to the incident polarization. Singly scattered light keeps its polarization; light that diffused in the underlying tissue has lost it and splits equally between the two channels:

I∥(λ) = S(λ) + ½ B(λ)     I⊥(λ) = ½ B(λ)     ΔI(λ) = I∥ − I⊥ = S(λ)

For one sphere, with amplitude functions S1, S2 and k = 2π nmedium/λ, averaging over the azimuth of an axially symmetric collection cone gives the residual

σΔ(λ, d) = (2π / k²) ∫ [ ¼ (|S1|² + cos²θ |S2|²) + ½ cosθ Re(S1S2*) ] sinθ dθ

integrated from 177.5° to 180° (a 2.5° half-angle cone, 6-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature, verified against 24 points). At exact backscattering it reduces to |S1(180°)|²/k². The signal at a probe position is the incoherent sum of σΔ over the nuclei under the probe, weighted by a Gaussian spot profile (σ = 13 µm). The spectrometer samples 400–800 nm every nanometre.

The diffuse background B(λ) is set to 25 times the single-scattering signal of a normal site, so that nuclei contribute a few percent of the detected light, as reported experimentally. Its shape (a smooth rise towards the red with hemoglobin-like absorption dips) is illustrative: it cancels in ΔI. What does not cancel is its photon noise. Both channels receive Poisson noise, and the exposure slider sets the number of detected photons per spectral channel.

4. Why the fringes encode size

For a soft sphere (m close to 1), backscattering is dominated by two weak reflections, at the front and at the back surface. They interfere with a phase difference 4π nnucleus d / λ, so the spectrum oscillates in 1/λ with a frequency proportional to the optical diameter. Between 400 and 800 nm the number of fringes is

N ≈ 2 nnucleus d (1/λmin − 1/λmax) ≈ 3.55 × d[µm]

about 20 for a 5.5 µm nucleus and 36 for a 10 µm one. This two-wave picture is only the explanation given in Learn mode; a unit test checks that the exact Mie spectra have this fringe count.

5. Inversion

The retrieved size distribution is a non-negative least-squares fit of the measured ΔI(λ) by a table of Mie spectra precomputed for 211 diameters from 4 to 14.5 µm at a nominal index m = 1.045:

ΔI(λ) ≈ Σk ck σΔ(λ, dk) + cubic baseline,   ck ≥ 0

where ck is the number of nuclei of diameter dk. A cubic polynomial is projected out of data and table, so only the fringes are fitted. The problem is solved greedily (matching pursuit, the CLEAN algorithm of radio astronomy): the table spectrum with the best single-component least-squares fit to the residual is selected, a fraction of it is subtracted, and the loop stops at three standard deviations of the photon noise. Nothing is reported unless the strongest component exceeds 4.5 σ. The size resolution set by the 400–800 nm bandwidth is about 0.3 µm.

The inversion never sees the simulation's inputs. Nuclei are simulated at m = 1.04 (normal) and m = 1.05 (dysplastic, hyperchromatic) on a 20 nm diameter grid, while the fit assumes m = 1.045 on a 50 nm grid. Because the fringe frequency depends on the product nnucleus·d, a 1% error on the index shifts the retrieved size by 1%. The Bench lets you see this by moving the index slider.

6. Limits of the model

  • Nuclei are not spheres. Real nuclei are spheroidal, inhomogeneous and rough. Their fringes are far less contrasted than those of ideal spheres, and real LSS spectra are correspondingly smoother. Scatter shows the mechanism in its cleanest form.
  • Large nuclei dominate. The backscattering cross-section grows faster than d², and with (m − 1)². Where populations are mixed, small nuclei are under-counted next to large ones, so the retrieved mean is biased upwards at the border of a lesion. This helps detection and hurts metrology; the "ground truth" toggle shows the size of the effect.
  • Crowded probes under-count. Nuclei closer in size than the 0.3 µm resolution merge into one component, and weak ones fall below the noise threshold.
  • Only nuclei scatter. Mitochondria and other small organelles, which add a smooth component to real spectra, are not simulated; neither are dispersion of the refractive indices, absorption in the nucleus, or coherent effects between neighbouring nuclei.
  • The tissue is a single layer seen en face, 800 × 600 µm, generated procedurally. Normal nuclei: 5.5 ± 0.45 µm. Dysplastic nuclei: 10 ± 1.3 µm, more closely packed.
